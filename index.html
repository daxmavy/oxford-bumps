How good is the bump model — and could it make money?

A gradient-boosted model that predicts every crew's chance of bumping, rowing over or being caught, scored only on years it never saw when it made the call.

See the live predictions board → · College strength, all-time →

A forecast is only worth something if its probabilities are honest and if acting on them pays. This page tests both: how closely the model's stated chances match what actually happened, across years it never trained on, and how it would have fared playing Fantasy Bumps, the prediction game where you buy and sell crews each day. It does well on the first and respectably on the second — and the gap between the two is the interesting part.

Are the percentages honest?

When it says 30%, it happens about 30% of the time

Each dot is a bucket of predictions; its height is how often the bump actually came. On the dashed line, the model means exactly what it says. We split it two ways — the seasons up to 2019 and the most recent four years — to show the calibration isn't an artefact of one era. Both sit on the line.

The same check for the other outcomes the model prices — a single bump, an over-bump of two or more, and rowing over. Over-bumps are rare, so that panel has few points, but where the model commits a probability, it holds.

Brier and the ranked probability score (RPS) are strictly proper scores — lower is better, and they can only be improved by being more honest, not more confident. "Climatology" is the score from knowing only the base rates by day; the model roughly halves the Brier. Intervals are 95% block-bootstrap, resampled by contest because a bump is one crew's gain and another's loss.

How far ahead it can see

Wednesday is hard; by Saturday it's read the field

Crews start seeded by last year's order, so day one pits each boat against the one most like it — close to a toss-up. Each day of racing sorts the genuinely quick from the rest, and the model's error falls accordingly.

Multiclass Brier score on each racing day (recent seasons), lower is better. The first day is the hardest call; the model sharpens as results come in.

Would it make money?

A top-decile bettor that can't quite beat the best human

We hand the model's probabilities to a simple optimiser that buys nine crew-shares per boat each day to maximise expected return, then replay the real Fantasy Bumps markets. Started at 2,000 crabs like everyone else, it finishes around of the single best human's score each year — comfortably top-decile of the field, but short of the champion. The dashes mark the perfect-foresight ceiling.

Why it can't catch the champion

The money is in the over-bumps — which nobody can predict

Splitting the returns by what the bought crew actually did is stark. The model earns almost all of its crabs from reliable single bumps. A perfect-foresight player earns nearly half from over-bumps — the +2 and +3 days when the crew ahead bumps out and you sail through. The model can't bet those: it only ever knows the ~13% odds, and at 13% an over-bump is a worse bet than a near-certain single bump. The champion catches them with knowledge of who's fast this week that no public data carries.

Total crabs earned over five seasons, split by the bought crew's result that day. The +2/+3 bars are where the model and a perfect player part company.

The honest ledger

The bumps it got wrong

Two ways a forecast loses you crabs. It can back a bump with confidence — staking real shares — and the bump doesn't come. Or it can wave away a valuable over-bump that then happens, leaving the payout on the table. Here are the worst of each, on years the model never saw.

How this works, and what it can't tell you

The model

For each crew on each racing day, a gradient-boosted classifier predicts a probability for every possible result — over-bumped, bumped, rowed over, bump, over-bump. Its inputs are where the crew starts, last year's finish, same-year Torpids form, squad continuity and Blues, within-week momentum, and — the part that matters most — the strength and form of the crew immediately ahead (its target) and behind (its chaser), recomputed every day. Unlike the interpretable model behind the live board, this one is tuned purely for accurate, well-calibrated probabilities, not for being explainable.

How it's scored

Strictly out of sample. To score any year, the model is trained only on earlier years and then predicts that year's racing — so every figure here is on data it never saw. Probabilities are graded with proper scoring rules (Brier and the ranked probability score), and calibration with reliability curves; uncertainty bands are block-bootstraps resampled by contest, because crews in a race aren't independent.

The betting test

The Fantasy Bumps engine is reconstructed exactly — crew prices, payouts and the daily buy/sell — and validated to the crab against the live site. Each day an integer program buys the nine shares per boat that maximise expected crabs under the model's probabilities. The "oracle" is the same optimiser given the actual results: the most any player could have made.

What it can't tell you

The model can't see who's injured, ill or quietly flying this week. That's exactly the edge the best human players have, and it's why the system tops out a notch below them — every other lever (the training objective, the loss, the risk appetite, even running several accounts) was tested and lands in the same place. "Blues" means the openweight Blue Boat only. Data: bumps.live and Anu Dudhia's archive (agreeing on 99.99% of 20,617 races), Fantasy Bumps, OURCs and Wikipedia.
