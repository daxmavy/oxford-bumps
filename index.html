Calibrated probabilistic forecasting of Oxford bumps racing and a market test of its limits

A gradient-boosted ordinal model, graded out of sample, produces calibrated outcome distributions for a coupled pursuit-race regatta; relational features supply most of the skill, and the forecasts reach the top decile of a real prediction market while remaining, on average, bounded by information the public record does not contain.

This manuscript and the underlying analysis were produced by an automated system under human direction. They are shared as a reproducible research exercise rather than a peer-reviewed publication; all figures regenerate from the primary data and have been checked against the sources.

Abstract

Bumps racing is a multi-day pursuit format in which crews start at fixed spacing along a narrow river and attempt to catch the crew ahead; each catch, or bump, exchanges two crews' positions for the following day, so the outcome of any single crew-day is one cell of a coupled permutation update rather than an independent event. Whether such a process can be forecast with calibrated probabilities, and whether those forecasts carry economic value, has not been established. We trained a gradient-boosted model to output a full distribution over each crew-day's signed places moved, and evaluated it under a strict leak-free walk-forward protocol on — out-of-sample crew-days of Oxford Summer Eights (2015–2019, 2022–2026). Features describing the crews immediately ahead and behind supplied most of this skill (paired difference in ranked probability score relative to an own-history-only model, —). Used to trade in the Fantasy Bumps prediction market, the forecasts placed in the top — of a field of several hundred entrants and reached — of the highest recorded human score, but exceeded it in no season and remained far below a perfect-foresight bound. The residual gap relative to perfect foresight was concentrated almost entirely in over-bumps—gains of two or more places that depend on a third crew's result and on crew speed absent from the public record—suggesting that the remaining limit on predictability here is plausibly informational rather than methodological, although residual model misspecification cannot be excluded.

Introduction

Bumps racing is the principal intercollegiate rowing format at Oxford and Cambridge. Because a river wide enough for side-by-side racing is unavailable, crews instead start in a line at fixed intervals and pursue one another: a crew that touches the crew ahead scores a bump, and the two crews exchange starting positions for the next day's racingA crew that bumps the boat ahead may not continue racing, so over a four-day regatta a single crew can rise by at most a few places by its own efforts. Larger single-day gains—over-bumps—occur when a crew further ahead is removed from the order, allowing the chasing crew to pass two or more positions at once. Summer Eights runs over four days; the closely related Torpids regatta uses different bumping conventions and is used here only as a predictor, not a prediction target.. A multi-day regatta therefore transforms an initial finishing order into a final one through a sequence of mechanically coupled pursuit outcomes, in which one crew's gain is by construction another crew's loss.

This structure makes per-crew outcomes statistically dependent within a day and links them across days, and it poses a well-defined forecasting problem: to estimate, given the order entering a day and everything known about the crews, the probability distribution over each crew's result. Two properties determine whether such a forecast is useful. The first is calibration—events issued probability p should occur a fraction p of the time—because only a calibrated forecast can be acted on without further correction. The second is economic value: the forecast should improve decisions under a realistic payoff, a more demanding test than any in-sample fit. Bumps racing affords an unusually direct measure of the second property, because Oxford operates a public prediction market, Fantasy Bumps, over the same races, in which participants trade shares in college crews each day and are paid when their crews advance.

Existing quantitative treatments of bumps racing emphasise interpretable strength ratings of crews and colleges rather than calibrated distributions over outcomes, and to our knowledge neither the calibration nor the realised market value of such forecasts has been reported under out-of-sample conditions. Here we (i) train a gradient-boosted ordinal model that outputs a full distribution over each crew-day's signed places moved; (ii) grade it under a leak-free walk-forward protocol using strictly proper scoring rules and reliability analysis, with confidence intervals from a block bootstrap that respects the within-contest coupling; (iii) quantify by ablation the contribution of relational features derived from neighbouring crews; and (iv) evaluate the forecasts as a trading strategy in Fantasy Bumps against the recorded human field and a perfect-foresight upper bound.

Predictability increases through the regatta

Discrimination between crews that advanced and crews that did not, measured by the area under the ROC curve for an upward move, increased monotonically across the four racing days, from — on the first day to — on the last (full model; Fig. 2). The first day is intrinsically the least predictable: crews are seeded by the previous year's finishing order, which has not yet re-sorted by current-season speed, so adjacent crews are on average the most evenly matched and the outcome is the hardest to call of the four days (day-1 area under the ROC curve approximately 0.69; Fig. 2). The relational features raised discrimination on every day, most markedly from the second day onward, once the realised results of earlier days have begun to separate genuinely fast crews from those merely well seeded (Fig. 2).

The forecasts have substantial but bounded market value

We evaluated the forecasts as a trading strategy in Fantasy Bumps by selecting, each day, the nine crew-shares per boat that maximise expected return under the model's probabilities subject to the budget, fully reconstructing the market's pricing and payout rules (Methods). It reached — of the single highest human score (range — across seasons) but exceeded it in no season, and fell well short of a perfect-foresight oracle (Fig. 3). The highest human score warrants caution as a benchmark: it is the maximum over several hundred entrants and is therefore inflated by sampling—as an extreme order statistic it overstates, by a margin attributable partly to chance, the score a strong entrant would reliably reproduce—so the champion ratio understates the strategy's standing relative to a typical strong player, while the percentile, which is insensitive to the extreme upper tail, is the more robust summary. With only five seasons the champion ratio additionally carries wide uncertainty.

The residual gap is concentrated in over-bumps

Decomposing realised returns by the actual result of the purchased crew localises almost the entire shortfall to the oracle within the over-bump classes (Fig. 4). The model earned essentially all of its return from single bumps, which it predicts well, whereas the perfect-foresight oracle earned — of its return from over-bumps—gains of two or more places, which occur when a crew further ahead is removed from the order, letting the chasing crew pass two or more positions at once. Over-bumps are both rare (— of crew-days) and, for the selection problem, dominated in expectation by near-certain single bumps, so an expected-value-maximising strategy forgoes them. The model retains some ability to discriminate over-bumps (AUC —, estimated from the few over-bumps in the out-of-sample period—approximately 2% of crew-days—and correspondingly uncertain), and remains calibrated where it commits probability (Fig. 1b), but not enough to render them favourable wagers.

Discussion

A gradient-boosted ordinal model produces calibrated and skilful out-of-sample forecasts of bumps-racing outcomes, and the relational structure of the format—the identity and state of the crews immediately ahead and behind—is the single largest measured increment of skill beyond a model using each crew's own history. The forecasts have substantial value in a real prediction market, placing on average in the top 7% of a field of several hundred (mean over five seasons, with a per-season range from about the top 2% to the top 17%), yet their value is bounded: across every intervention we examined, including alternative training objectives, risk-adjusted crew selection, and a diversified multi-account portfolio, performance converged on roughly — of the single best human score rather than exceeding it (Methods).

We interpret this bound as informational rather than methodological. The return that separates even a strong forecaster from the oracle is concentrated in over-bumps, whose occurrence depends on the result of a third crew and on within-week changes in crew speed—injury, illness, substitutions, training form—that are not represented in the public record. The strongest human competitors plausibly hold such private information. Three observations support this reading: the residual is localised to precisely the outcomes that depend on unobserved speed (Fig. 4); independent interventions converge on a common ceiling rather than improving incrementally; and the benchmark itself, the field maximum, is upward-biased as an order statistic. We nonetheless cannot exclude residual model misspecification, and the market evaluation rests on only five seasons, so the quantitative ceiling should be read as approximate.

Several limitations qualify these results. Membership of an Oxford openweight crew ("Blues") is recorded only for the first boat; reserve and lightweight representation is unavailable, and an attempt to incorporate external athlete-pedigree records added no measurable signal (Methods). The perfect-foresight bound for the 2022 season is a slight underestimate because a small number of that season's crews could not be matched unambiguously across data sources. More broadly, the interventions we tested converged on a common ceiling rather than improving incrementally, consistent with the forecasts capturing much of the predictive signal recoverable from the public record, though we cannot exclude residual misspecification; closing the remaining gap would require information that is, by the nature of the sport, revealed only on the day.

Methods

Data

The unit of analysis is a crew-day: one college crew on one of the four racing days, with outcome equal to the signed number of places it moved that day. Results were drawn from two independently compiled records of every Oxford bumps race, which agree on 99.99% of the 20,617 races they jointly cover back to 1826The historical archive maintained by A. Dudhia and the live results service bumps.live; the latter additionally provides crew, cox and coach listings from 2007 onward. Crew compositions and Blue-Boat membership were cross-referenced against published Boat Race records.. Although the records extend over two centuries, the model was fitted on the modern era only (1996 onward, Summer Eights). Pre-1900 results, which follow a different convention, composite crews not attributable to a single college, and a small number of records with implausibly large single-day moves were excluded. The same-year Torpids regatta (rowed in the preceding academic term) was used as a predictor of current-season strength. Records from different sources were joined by season, gender, college and boat number. The market evaluation used the live Fantasy Bumps order books, daily results, and final leaderboards for 2022–2026.

Outcome representation and model

Each crew-day's signed places moved was discretised into six ordered classes—a multi-place loss (two or more places down), a bump down (−1), a row-over (0), a bump up (+1), an over-bump up (+2), and a multi-place over-bump up (+3 or more), the downward losses of two or more places being pooled into one class because they are individually rare; throughout, "over-bump" without qualification denotes an upward gain—and the model issues a probability for each. Treating the outcome as ordinal rather than nominal is required for the ranked probability score to penalise mass placed far from the truth. The predictor is a histogram-based gradient-boosting classifier (300 trees, learning rate 0.05, maximum 15 leaves per tree, minimum 40 samples per leaf, L2 regularisation 1.0), which accommodates the many interacting and frequently missing inputs without manual transformation. Hyperparameters were fixed in advance and not tuned against the test seasons. This model is intended to produce calibrated probabilities and is distinct from the interpretable strength-rating model published separately (live predictions; ratings).

Features

Own-crew features comprise starting position, division and field size; the previous year's finishing position, net places gained, and blades; a three-year form trend; same-year Torpids finish, bumps and blades; the retained-rower fraction and squad continuity; the number of Blue-Boat oarsmen; and within-week state (places gained so far and whether the crew bumped or was bumped on the preceding day)Net places gained is the signed sum of a crew's daily moves over a regatta; blades denotes bumping on every racing day; the retained-rower fraction is the proportion of a crew's nine seats filled by rowers who raced for the same crew in the previous year, and squad continuity is a related measure of roster overlap between consecutive years.. Relational features mirror these for the crews immediately ahead (the bump target) and immediately behind (the chaser), recomputed from the current order each day, together with signed differences between a crew and each neighbour and a wider local-strength summary over the next one to three positions. The ablation in Table 1 compares the full feature set with the own-crew subset.

Walk-forward evaluation and leakage control

All reported metrics are out of sample under a walk-forward protocol: to forecast a given season the model was trained only on crew-days from strictly earlier seasons, after which the window advanced by one year. Within a race week the model may use the realised results of earlier days—information genuinely available to any forecaster by that morning—but never the current day's own outcome; relational features are rebuilt each day from the standings to date. Same-year Torpids is admissible because it precedes Summer Eights by an academic term. Fixing the hyperparameters in advance avoids re-introducing leakage through model selection on the test seasons.

Scoring, calibration and discrimination

Probabilistic accuracy was assessed with two strictly proper scoring rules, the multiclass Brier score and the ranked probability score, and with skill scores relative to a climatology that predicts the class frequencies of the training years conditional on racing day, refit at each walk-forward step. Calibration of each named event was summarised by the expected calibration error (count-weighted mean absolute deviation between predicted and observed frequency over deciles of the forecast) and by the Cox calibration slope (the coefficient of a logistic regression of the outcome on the forecast logit, where unity is ideal). The expected calibration error and slope quoted in the text refer to the probability of an upward move, and deciles are equal-count quantile bins. Discrimination was summarised by the area under the ROC curve, computed one-versus-rest for the relevant binary event (for example, an upward move). Confidence intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a block bootstrap resampling whole contests (year × gender × day × division), because crew-days within a contest are mechanically coupled; paired model comparisons resample the same clusters for both models. Reported intervals use 2,000 bootstrap replicates.

Market reconstruction and crew selection

Fantasy Bumps prices each crew by its position on the river and pays a holding when its crew rows over or advancesA row-over returns 7% of a crew's value and each place gained a further 14%, so a single bump returns 21% and a two-place over-bump 35%; the head-of-the-river crew's row-over returns 17.5% instead of 7%, compensating for its inability to bump upward. A bumped crew loses the value it sheds. The reconstructed engine reproduces the market's own published payouts exactly for every crew of every season evaluated. The in-game currency is denominated in units the game terms "crabs".. Each participant manages a men's and a women's boat, each endowed with 1,000 units and holding nine shares, with the markets reopening between racing days; share prices are fixed functions of river position, from approximately 300 units for the head of the river to about 20 for the lowest crew. The selection rule chooses, each day, how to allocate the nine shares across crews so as to maximise the total expected return—the sum over crews of the number of shares placed on a crew times that crew's expected per-share return under the model's probabilities—subject to the total cost at the posted prices not exceeding the budget, with the share counts non-negative integers summing to nine; this integer program is solved exactly. Because holdings are fully liquidated and rebuilt each day and the daily return factors are independent across days given the standings, maximising each day's expected return also maximises expected final wealth, so this myopic rule is optimal for the stated objective. The oracle applies the same selection rule with the realised outcomes substituted for the forecasts, giving the maximum attainable return. The strategy's standing in the field is summarised by its percentile—the fraction of recorded human final scores it exceeds—and by its ratio to the highest recorded human score of the season (the field maximum, referred to throughout as the champion).

Robustness and rejected interventions

Several interventions intended to raise market performance were tested and did not improve out-of-sample results: up-weighting the training loss toward high-value events; a risk-adjusted (mean–variance) selection objective, which reduced expected return because the wagers are small relative to the bankroll and so incur little volatility cost to recover; and a multi-account portfolio requiring only one of several accounts to perform well, which—even with several hundred accounts placing deliberately diversified high-variance wagers—did not exceed roughly 86% of the champion, because every account can only wager on crews the model already rates highly, whereas the decisive over-bumps are precisely those it rates low. Two external data sources—OURCs Rowing-On qualification times and individual-rower career ratings assembled from ~22,000 rowers—were merged and tested under a controlled comparison and found to add no robust signal, and are excluded from the reported model.
