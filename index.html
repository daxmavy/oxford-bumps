Forecasting Oxford bumps racing: a coherent joint model matches independent marginals, and the market ceiling is set by unpredictable over-bumps

Bumps racing produces a mechanically coupled set of outcomes each day. We ask whether modelling that coupling explicitly improves the forecast, whether the rare high-value events can be predicted, and how much of a real prediction market a calibrated forecaster can win.

This manuscript and the underlying analysis were produced by an automated system under human direction, and were revised through several rounds of independent automated peer review. It is shared as a reproducible research exercise rather than a peer-reviewed publication; all figures regenerate from the primary data and have been checked against the sources.

In brief. Bumps racing is decided by a chain of crews catching one another, so the day's results are linked rather than independent. We compared two ways to forecast it—one that ignores the linkage and one that simulates it—and asked how well either fares in the real betting game run on the races. The linkage-aware model turns out to be a statistical dead heat with the simpler one and no better at the rare big jumps (over-bumps); and while a near-perfect forecaster would beat the typical player every year, it almost never beats the very best, because over-bumps hinge on events that no pre-race information reveals. A calibrated model extracts what the public record allows; the last few per cent belong to the river.

Abstract

In bumps racing, college crews start in a line and chase one another up a river; catching the crew ahead—a bump—exchanges the two crews' positions for the next day. Outcomes within a day are therefore mechanically coupled, which raises a natural question: does a model that respects this coupling forecast better than one that does not? We compared, under an identical leak-free walk-forward over — out-of-sample crew-days of Oxford Summer Eights, an independent per-crew gradient-boosted classifier of the six-class ordinal outcome against a coherent generative model that simulates the coupled catch process from latent crew strengths. The generative model is more internally coherent yet cannot rank the rare over-bump (a gain of two or more places). Both models badly under-predict over-bumps; the marginal model nonetheless discriminates which crews are candidates, so a post-hoc recalibration restores the over-bump probability mass (from — to — of the observed frequency) at no cost to aggregate accuracy. Played in the Fantasy Bumps market, the forecasts reach — of the best human score but never beat that champion across five seasons; a Monte-Carlo of the strategy's own holdings puts the chance of beating it below — under any strategy, and recalibrating the tail or seeking variance helps only marginally. The market ceiling is therefore informational—set by which crew will over-bump, an event neither model can foresee from the public record—rather than a fixable modelling defect.

Introduction

Bumps racing is the principal intercollegiate rowing format at Oxford and Cambridge. Because the river is too narrow for side-by-side racing, crews instead set off in a line at fixed spacing and pursue one another; a crew that catches the crew ahead scores a bump, and the two exchange starting positions for the following dayA crew that bumps may not continue, so by its own efforts a crew rises at most one place per day. Larger single-day gains—over-bumps of two or more places—occur when a crew further ahead is removed from the order, leaving the chasing crew to pass the vacated positions. Summer Eights runs over four days; the related Torpids regatta uses different conventions and enters here only as a predictor.. A four-day regatta thus transforms an initial order into a final one through a sequence of coupled permutation updates, in which one crew's gain is by construction another's loss.

This coupling poses a concrete modelling choice. One option is to predict each crew-day's outcome with an independent classifier; this is flexible and easy to calibrate marginally, but the stacked marginals need not form a valid joint over a contest's permutation, and they have no mechanism for generating over-bumps. The other is a generative model of the race itself—latent crew strengths driving a simulated catch process—which is coherent by construction and produces over-bumps endogenously, at the cost of a rigid functional form. Which serves the stated objective better: to forecast each crew-day's outcome as accurately and as well-calibrated as possible (so that, when the model says 30%, the event happens about 30% of the time), and to perform well in the public Fantasy Bumps prediction market run over the same races?

Here we (i) implement both model classes and grade them on identical out-of-sample crew-days with strictly proper scoring rules and reliability analysis, with confidence intervals from a block bootstrap that respects the within-contest coupling; (ii) quantify the incoherence of the marginal forecast and the discrimination and calibration of the rare over-bump class; (iii) test whether post-hoc recalibration of that class changes either accuracy or market value; and (iv) characterise the market as the tournament it is, reporting the probability of beating the field's best human across selection strategies rather than a single expected-value operating point.

Discussion

Two questions motivated this work, and the data answer both cleanly. First, does respecting the coupled structure of the race improve the forecast? No: a coherent generative simulator and an incoherent stack of independent marginals are statistically indistinguishable in discrimination, and the simpler marginal model is in fact slightly better on proper scores and far better on the rare tail. Coherence is a real property—the simulator's contest-level conservation residual is a quarter of the marginal model's—but it did not translate into predictive value here, and we would caution against assuming it must.

Second, what limits performance in the market? The separation between a strong forecaster and a perfect one is concentrated entirely in over-bumps, and our results locate the difficulty precisely. The relational model already knows which crews are over-bump candidates (it ranks them with AUC well above chance); what it cannot do is assign them enough probability, and that is a calibration defect we fixed. Yet fixing it barely moves the market outcome, because an over-bump requires a third crew to be removed at the right moment—for example, the crew two places ahead must catch its own target and pull over early, opening a gap the chasing crew can reach—a contingency that turns on a different race unfolding a particular way, minutes up the river, which no pre-race information we have access to determines. After recalibration the strategy does occasionally back these crews, lifting its probability of beating the champion, but only into the low single figures. No selection strategy we tried—expected-value, variance-seeking, or concentrated—reliably wins, and the champion's score is not an artefact of being the maximum of a large field. We therefore read the ceiling as informational rather than methodological: the model extracts essentially all of the publicly available signal, and the residual is the genuinely unpredictable timing of over-bumps, supplemented by whatever private knowledge of current crew speed the strongest competitors hold.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The market evaluation rests on five seasons of one game at one venue, so its headline figures carry wide season-to-season ranges, which we report rather than average away. The over-bump analysis rests on — events, so its calibration estimates are correspondingly uncertain (we attach intervals throughout). The generative model is one specific mechanistic form; a different simulator might generate over-bumps more readily, though it would have to do so without harming the pairwise calibration that matches the common case. And membership of an Oxford openweight crew is recorded only for the first boat. The honest summary is that, for this system, a flexible marginal model with a recalibrated tail is the better forecaster, modelling the coupling explicitly does not help, and the market's last few per cent are bought with information the river only reveals on the day.

Methods

Data

The unit is a crew-day: one college crew on one of the four racing days, with outcome the signed places it moved. Results come from two independently compiled records of every Oxford bumps race, which agree on 99.99% of the 20,617 races they jointly coverThe historical archive of A. Dudhia and the live results service bumps.live; records were joined across sources by season, gender, college and boat number. A small number of 2022 crews that could not be matched cause that season's perfect-foresight bound to be a slight underestimate.. The modern era (1996 onward, Summer Eights) is used for the marginal model; the generative model's roster graph and the Hilary-term Torpids predictor begin in 2007. Pre-1900 results, composite crews, and records with implausibly large moves are excluded. The market evaluation uses the live Fantasy Bumps order books, daily results and leaderboards for 2022–2026.

Two model classes

The marginal model is a histogram-based gradient-boosting classifier predicting, for each crew-day independently, a probability over six ordered classes (over-bumped, bumped, rowed over, bump, over-bump, multi-place over-bump). It uses each crew's own history and seeding plus relational features describing the crews immediately ahead and behind, recomputed from the current order each day; hyperparameters were fixed in advance. (Additional inputs—Torpids form, individual-rower ratings, qualification times—were tested in earlier work and found non-additive, and are excluded.) The coherent joint model assigns each crew a latent skill in boat-gaps of pace (a Bayesian estimate from seeding, Torpids, last year and Blue-Boat membership, updated within the week by the observed earlier days) and simulates the coupled catch process: each crew rows at its skill plus daily noise, chases the nearest crew ahead, and both stop on a catch, so over-bumps arise endogenously when a crew further ahead is removed. Monte-Carlo over the skill posterior and the day noise yields a coherent six-class marginal per crew-day. The day-noise scale is calibrated so the simulated pairwise bump rate matches the data.

Leak-free evaluation

Both models are graded under an identical walk-forward: to forecast year Y each is fitted only on data strictly before Y, and within a race week day d conditions on the realised results of days < d (never the current day). For the marginal model, hyperparameters were fixed a priori; for the joint model, the skill hyperparameters are refit each year on earlier seasons. Proper scores are the multiclass Brier score and the ranked probability score; skill scores are relative to a climatology refit each step (day-only, and a stronger variant additionally conditioned on river-position quintile). Calibration is summarised by reliability curves, the expected calibration error, and the Cox calibration slope (the coefficient of a logistic regression of outcome on forecast logit; unity is ideal). Discrimination is the one-versus-rest ROC AUC. Confidence intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a block bootstrap of 1,000 replicates that resamples whole contests (year × gender × day × division), the independent unit given the within-day coupling; paired model comparisons resample the same clusters.

Recalibration and the coherence diagnostic

Because the walk-forward predictions are already out of sample, the marginal model's probabilities are recalibrated leak-free without retraining: to recalibrate year Y, a per-class logistic (Platt) map is fit on the out-of-sample predictions of the test years strictly before Y and applied to year Y, then the six classes are renormalised; the earliest year keeps its raw probabilities. The coherence diagnostic is the per-contest sum of expected signed places moved. Because class probabilities are mapped to places through class-mean magnitudes, even a perfect forecast leaves a nonzero binning residual, so we report the marginal model's residual against that perfect-foresight floor rather than against zero.

Market reconstruction and decision frontier

Fantasy Bumps prices each crew by its river position and pays a holding when its crew rows over or advancesA row-over returns 7% of a crew's value and each place gained a further 14% (so a single bump returns 21% and a two-place over-bump 35%); the head crew's row-over returns 17.5%. The reconstructed engine reproduces the platform's published payouts exactly. Participants manage a men's and a women's boat, each endowed with 1,000 units holding nine shares, rebuilt daily.. Each day a strategy chooses the nine shares per boat that maximise its objective subject to the budget, solved as an integer program; the expected return uses within-class conditional mean places, so a multi-place over-bump is valued at its empirical +3.6 rather than the class edge (these two open-class magnitudes are estimated over the full sample; with only 62 multi-place over-bumps the effect on the headline figures is negligible). We report a grid of strategies—expected value, three degrees of variance-seeking, and concentration—for each model. The expected-value rule maximises expected, not realised, wealth, and because the budget binds it is not the optimal policy for a contest decided by an extreme order statistic. To probe that gap we report a within-model diagnostic: holding each strategy's realised-path picks fixed, we redraw their returns from the model's own class probabilities and estimate the chance the resulting final wealth exceeds the (fixed) champion score. This is a variance diagnostic, not a true tournament-win probability—it neither lets the picks adapt nor models the opposing field—so we read it alongside, not above, the realised season ranks.
