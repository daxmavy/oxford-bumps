Forecasting Oxford Summer Eights bumps: calibration and betting performance

A leak-free probabilistic model, evaluated out of sample with proper scoring rules and tested as a Fantasy Bumps bettor.

Abstract

We train a gradient-boosted classifier to predict, for every crew on every racing day of Oxford Summer Eights, a probability for each possible result — over-bumped, bumped, rowed over, bump, over-bump. The model is evaluated strictly out of sample: to score any season it is trained only on earlier seasons. Its probabilities are well-calibrated and skilful by proper scoring rules — a Brier score of — against — for predicting base rates alone, with a mean calibration error near —, and the calibration holds on both a historical and a recent window. Used to play Fantasy Bumps through an exactly-reconstructed market, the model reaches about — of the single best human's score each season and ranks in the top — of entrants, but does not beat the champion. The residual gap is attributable almost entirely to over-bumps, high-value events that are not predictable at the crew level.

1 Objective

A probabilistic forecast is useful only if two things hold: its stated probabilities are honest — when it says 30%, the event happens about 30% of the time — and acting on them is profitable. This report evaluates both for a model of Oxford bumps racing. The first is a question of calibration and accuracy, measured with strictly proper scoring rules. The second is a question of economic value, measured by playing Fantasy Bumps, a public prediction market in which players buy and sell college crews each day and are paid when their crews bump or row over. Throughout, the benchmark is not a textbook baseline but real human players and a perfect-foresight upper bound.

2 Data

The unit of analysis is a crew-day: one college boat on one of the four racing days, whose outcome is the signed number of places it moved. Results come from two independent records of every Oxford bumps race — Anu Dudhia's archive and bumps.live — which agree on 99.99% of the 20,617 races they both cover, back to 1826; crew lists, coxes and Blues come from bumps.live and Wikipedia. Modelling is restricted to the modern era (1996 onward), Summer Eights only, with same-year Torpids used as a predictor. The betting evaluation uses the live Fantasy Bumps markets for 2022–2026 — crew prices, daily results and the final human leaderboards — and the Rowing-On and finishing-order documents published by OURCs. After exclusions the out-of-sample evaluation covers — crew-days across the seasons 2015–2019 and 2022–2026 (2020–21 were cancelled).

3 Methodology

3.1  Model and outcome

For each crew-day the target is the day's places-moved, grouped into ordinal classes — over-bumped (≤ −2), bumped (−1), rowed over (0), bump (+1), over-bump (+2), multi-place over-bump (≥ +3). A gradient-boosted decision-tree classifier (histogram-based, multinomial) predicts a probability for each class. Unlike the interpretable skill-and-simulation model behind the live board, this model is optimised purely for accurate, well-calibrated probabilities, not for being explainable.

3.2  Features

Each crew-day carries its own attributes — starting position and division, last year's finish, same-year Torpids form, squad continuity and Blue Boat members, and within-week momentum (places gained so far, whether it bumped the day before). The decisive additions are relational: the strength and recent form of the crew immediately ahead (its bump target) and immediately behind (its chaser), together with how far it out-rates the boats two and three places ahead, all recomputed from the current order each day. Bumps are inherently relational — a crew goes up only if it is faster than the boat in front — so these features carry most of the signal.

3.3  Training methodology

Evaluation is a strict walk-forward: to predict a given season, the model is fitted only on crew-days from earlier seasons, so every figure in this report is on data the model never saw when it made the call. Within a week, day-d predictions are allowed to condition on the realised results of days 1…d−1 — information any forecaster would have — but never on the day's own outcome; the relational features above are rebuilt each morning from the standings to date. Hyper-parameters are fixed in advance (300 trees, depth-limited, learning rate 0.05, leaf regularisation) rather than tuned to the test seasons, to avoid fitting the evaluation. No class re-weighting is used; experiments that up-weighted rare high-value events or weighted the loss by stake-at-risk did not improve out-of-sample accuracy and were discarded.

3.4  Evaluation of the probabilities

Probabilities are graded with strictly proper scoring rules — the multiclass Brier score and the ordinal ranked probability score (RPS), both of which reward honesty rather than confidence — and compared to climatology, a baseline that predicts only the base rates by racing day. Calibration is assessed with reliability curves (predicted probability versus observed frequency), the expected calibration error, and the calibration slope. Uncertainty intervals are 95% block-bootstraps resampled by contest, because the crews in a single race are not independent — one crew's bump is another's being caught.

3.5  The betting test

The Fantasy Bumps engine is reconstructed exactly — positional crew prices, the payout schedule (7% of value for a row-over, +14% per place gained; 17.5% for a headship row-over) and the daily buy/sell — and validated to the crab against the live site on every crew of every recent season. Each day, an integer program selects the nine crew-shares per boat that maximise expected crab return under the model's probabilities, subject to the budget; with full daily liquidation this myopic rule is optimal for expected final wealth. The oracle is the same optimiser given the actual results — the most any player could have made — and the human benchmark is the recorded Fantasy Bumps leaderboard. A risk-adjusted (mean-variance) objective and a multi-account portfolio were both tested; neither improved on expected-return maximisation (see §5).

4.4  Where the residual gap lies

Splitting realised returns by what the purchased crew actually did (Figure 5) localises the entire gap to the oracle. The model earns almost all of its crabs from reliable single bumps. A perfect-foresight player earns nearly half from over-bumps — the +2 and +3 days when the crew ahead bumps out. The model cannot bet those: it only ever knows the ~13% base odds, and at 13% an over-bump is a worse expected bet than a near-certain single bump. Capturing that value requires knowing which crew over-bumps, which is information no public data carries.

5 Limitations and the performance ceiling

Beyond the central finding, several limitations bound the result. The model cannot observe who is injured, ill or in unusually good form this week — precisely the edge the best human players hold. Every alternative lever we tested converged on the same ceiling: re-weighting the training loss toward high-value events, a risk-adjusted (mean-variance) selection objective, and a multi-account portfolio that needs only one account to win all left performance at, or below, expected-return maximisation. "Blues" counts the openweight Blue Boat only; reserve and lightweight squad membership is not in the data, and a scrape of external athlete records did not add usable signal. One numerical caveat: the 2022 oracle is a slight under-estimate because a handful of that season's crews could not be matched across data sources.
