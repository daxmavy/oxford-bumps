Forecasting Oxford Summer Eights bumps

Bumps racing is Oxford's strangest annual spectacle: college boats chase each other up a narrow river, and catching the boat ahead — a bump — moves you up the order for the next day. This report asks two questions of a statistical model of that racing. Can it predict the bumps, with honest probabilities? And would acting on those predictions make money?

This report, the model and the analysis were produced by an AI system directed by a human. Treat it as a research experiment shared for interest, not a peer-reviewed result. Every figure is reproducible from the raw data, and the workings are checked against the sources throughout.

Abstract. We train a gradient-boosted model to predict, for every crew on every racing day, a probability for each possible result — over-bumped, bumped, rowed over, bump, over-bump. It is graded strictly out of sample: to score any season the model sees only earlier seasons. Its probabilities prove well-calibrated and skilful — a Brier score of — against — for predicting base rates alone, a mean calibration error near —, and the calibration holds on both an older and a recent window. Set loose on Fantasy Bumps — a real prediction market where players trade college crews each day — through an exactly-reconstructed engine, the model reaches about — of the single best human's score each season and ranks in the top — of entrants, but never beats the champion. The reason it cannot is the most interesting result, and it takes the rest of the report to earn.

Objective

A forecast is worth something only if two things are true. Its probabilities must be honest: when it says a crew has a 30% chance of going up, that should happen about 30% of the time, no more and no less. And acting on them must pay: a forecast that is right on average but useless for any decision is a curiosity, not a tool. This report tests both claims for a model of bumps racing, and is careful to test them against demanding benchmarks rather than easy ones.

Calibration and accuracy we measure with strictly proper scoring rules — scores that a forecaster can improve only by being more honest, never by bluffing. Economic value we measure the hard way, by playing Fantasy Bumps: a public game, run alongside the real regatta, in which players are handed a budget of crabsThe game's currency, 🦀. Each player manages a men's and a women's boat of nine shares and starts each with 1,000 crabs; the markets lock 30 minutes before racing and reopen afterwards, so you rebuild your holdings every day. and buy shares in the college crews they think will climb, selling and re-buying as the week unfolds. The benchmark there is not a textbook strawman but the recorded scores of hundreds of real players, and a perfect-foresight upper bound on what anyone could have won.

Data

The unit of analysis is a crew-day: one college boat on one of the four racing days, whose outcome is the signed number of places it moved that day. Results come from two records of every Oxford bumps race, compiled independently and in close agreementAnu Dudhia's long-running archive and the live results service bumps.live. Cross-checked, they agree on 99.99% of the 20,617 races they both cover, back to 1826 — a reassuring sign that the underlying results are right. bumps.live additionally supplies crew lists, coxes and coaches from 2007 on.. Crew compositions and Oxford Blues come from the same source and from Wikipedia's Boat Race tables.

Although the records reach back two centuries, the model is fitted only on the modern era — 1996 onward, Summer Eights, with the Hilary-term Torpids regatta used purely as a same-year predictorPre-1900 results use a different convention and are excluded; so are composite boats that can't be attributed to a single college, and the rare data artefacts with implausibly large moves. Summer Eights and Torpids share the bumps format but differ in their rules, so only Eights is predicted.. The betting evaluation draws on the live Fantasy Bumps markets for 2022–2026 — crew prices, daily results and the final human leaderboards — together with the Rowing-On qualification and finishing-order documents published by OURCs. After exclusions, the out-of-sample evaluation covers — crew-days across the seasons 2015–2019 and 2022–2026; the 2020 and 2021 regattas were cancelled.

Methodology

The model

For each crew-day the model predicts a probability for every possible result, treating the day's places-moved as an ordered outcomeThe classes, in order, are: over-bumped (down two or more), bumped (−1), rowed over (0), bump (+1), over-bump (+2), and a multi-place over-bump (+3 or more). Treating them as ordinal rather than as unrelated labels matters for the ranked probability score, which then penalises a forecast for putting weight far from the truth — predicting a +3 surge when the crew rowed over is charged more than predicting a single bump.. The predictor is a gradient-boosted ensemble of decision trees, which handles the many interacting, sometimes-missing inputs without hand-tuning. This is a deliberately different object from the interpretable skill-and-simulation model behind the live predictions board: that one is built to be explainable, this one purely to be accurate and well-calibrated.

Features

Each crew-day carries its own history — where it starts the week, how it finished last year, its same-year Torpids form, how much of its squad it kept, any Blue Boat oarsmen aboard, and its momentum so far this week. On their own these say surprisingly little, because a bump is not a solo effort but a race against a specific opponent. The features that carry most of the signal are therefore relational: the strength and recent form of the crew immediately ahead — the one you are chasing — and of the crew immediately behind, the one chasing you, recomputed from the live order each day, along with how far you out-rate the boats two and three places up the riverThe full set: own start position, division and field size; last year's finish, net places and blades; three-year form trend; same-year Torpids finish, bumps and blades; retained-rower fraction and squad continuity; Blue Boat count; within-week places-so-far and whether it bumped or was bumped yesterday. The relational block mirrors all of these for the crews ahead and behind, plus signed differences (you-minus-target, you-minus-chaser) and a wider "local strength" read over the next few boats. Adding the relational block is the single biggest improvement over a crew's own features, lifting day-2 discrimination from roughly 0.79 to 0.87.. Intuitively, you go up if and only if you are faster than the boat in front, so the model needs to know who that boat is and how good it is.

Training and out-of-sample protocol

Everything in this report is scored on data the model never saw when it made the call. The evaluation is a strict walk-forward: to predict a given season, the model is fitted only on crew-days from earlier seasons, then asked to forecast the new one cold; the window then rolls forward a year and repeats. This is the discipline that separates a real forecast from hindsight, and it is easy to get subtly wrongThe most common leak is letting a feature peek at the future. Within a race week the model is allowed to use the realised results of earlier days — information any forecaster genuinely has by Thursday morning — but never the current day's own outcome, and the relational features are rebuilt each morning from the standings to date. Same-year Torpids is fair game because it is rowed in Hilary term, months before Eights. Hyper-parameters were fixed in advance — 300 trees, shallow, a learning rate of 0.05, with leaf and L2 regularisation — rather than tuned against the test seasons, which would quietly re-introduce the leak.. We resisted the temptation to tune the model against the very seasons we then report on; several plausible tweaks that improved one window turned out to be noise across all of themUp-weighting rare high-value events, weighting the loss by the crabs at stake, and adding an explicit "second-in-division can double-bump" flag each helped a narrow subgroup but did not improve the out-of-sample Brier score overall, so none were kept..

Grading the probabilities

Probabilities are graded with two strictly proper scoring rules — the multiclass Brier score and the ordinal ranked probability scoreThe Brier score is the mean squared error between the predicted probability vector and the one-hot truth; the ranked probability score is the same idea applied to the cumulative distribution, so it respects the ordering of the outcomes. Both are minimised, in expectation, only by reporting your true beliefs — there is no reward for false confidence. — and against climatology, a baseline that knows only the base rates of each outcome by racing day and nothing about who is rowingThe term is borrowed from weather forecasting, where a "climatological" forecast predicts the historical average for the date and ignores today's conditions. It is the no-skill floor every real model must clear; here it gives every crew on a given day the same forecast — it cannot even tell that the Head of the River is unable to bump upwards.. Calibration itself we read from reliability curves — predicted probability against observed frequency — summarised by the expected calibration error and the calibration slope. Uncertainty intervals are 95% block-bootstraps, resampled by contest rather than by crew, because the crews in a single race are not independent: one crew's bump is, by definition, another crew's being caught.

The betting test

To turn probabilities into money we reconstruct the Fantasy Bumps market exactly and feed it the model's forecasts. Crews are priced purely by their position on the river, and a holding pays out when its crew rows over or climbsA row-over pays 7% of the crew's value and each place gained a further 14%, so a single bump returns about 21% and an over-bump of two about 35%; the head crew's row-over is worth 17.5% to compensate for the fact that it cannot bump. A bumped crew simply loses the value it sheds. The reconstructed engine reproduces the live site's own published payout figures to the crab for every crew of every recent season.. Each day, an optimiser buys the nine shares per boat that maximise expected crab return under the model's probabilities, within the budgetFormally an integer program: choose how many of the nine shares to place on each crew to maximise expected return subject to cost. Because you fully liquidate and rebuild every day, maximising each day's expected return also maximises expected final wealth, so this myopic rule is optimal — a fact we lean on when testing risk-adjusted and multi-account variants later.. Against this we set two yardsticks: the recorded human leaderboard, and an oracle — the same optimiser handed the actual results, i.e. the most any player could conceivably have won.

Results

The probabilities are honest

The model's stated chances track what actually happens, and they do so on both an early window (2015–2019) and a recent one (2022–2026), which rules out the calibration being a quirk of a single era. In Figure 1 the points sit on the line of perfect calibration; Figure 2 shows the same for the individual outcomes it prices. Numerically it roughly halves the error of climatology (Table 1).

It sees further as the week unfolds

The first day is the hardest call. Crews are seeded by the previous year's order, so on Wednesday each boat races the one most like itself, and the result is close to a coin toss. Each subsequent day of racing sorts the genuinely quick from the merely well-seeded, and the model's error falls accordingly (Figure 3).

As a bettor: top-decile, but not the champion

Started at the same 2,000 crabs as every entrant, the model's optimiser clears the field median every season and reaches roughly — of the single best human's score, placing it comfortably in the top decile of players (Figure 4). But it never wins. It does not beat the champion in any season, and it falls a long way short of the perfect-foresight oracle. That gap, not the respectable headline, is where the interesting physics is.

The gap is all over-bumps — and nobody can predict those

Splitting the realised returns by what the purchased crew actually did (Figure 5) localises the entire shortfall to the oracle. The model earns almost all of its crabs from reliable single bumps. A perfect player earns nearly half from over-bumps — the +2 and +3 days when the crew ahead bumps out and you sail through two places at once. The model cannot bet those, and not for want of trying: an over-bump happens about 13% of the time even for a well-placed crew, and at 13% it is simply a worse expected bet than a near-certain single bump, so the optimiser correctly passes it up. The champion catches them with private knowledge of who is quietly flying this week — exactly the information no public dataset carries.

The two ways a forecast bleeds crabs are worth seeing by name. It can back a bump with confidence — staking real shares — and the bump never comes (Table 2). Or it can wave away a valuable over-bump that then happens, leaving the payout on the table (Table 3). These are the worst of each, all out of sample.

Limitations and the ceiling

Several limits bound the result. The model cannot see who is injured, ill, or in unusually good form this week — precisely the edge the best human players hold, and the reason the system settles a notch below them. Every alternative lever we tried converged on the same ceiling rather than breaking through itTested and discarded: re-weighting the training loss toward high-value events; a risk-adjusted (mean-variance) selection objective, which only ever lost expected value because the bets are small relative to the bankroll, so there is little volatility drag to recover; and a multi-account portfolio that needs only one of several accounts to win — even with 400 accounts placing diverse risky bets it topped out near 85% of the champion, because all accounts can only bet on crews the model already rates highly, and the winning over-bumps are exactly the ones it rates low.. "Blues" counts the openweight Blue Boat only; reserve and lightweight membership is not in the data, and a manual scrape of external athlete records added no usable signal. One numerical caveat: the 2022 oracle is a slight under-estimate, because a handful of that season's crews could not be matched cleanly across the data sources.

The honest summary is that the model extracts close to all of the publicly predictable signal in bumps racing, which is enough to forecast it honestly and to out-bet most humans — but not enough to beat the best, whose edge lives in information the river only reveals on the day.
