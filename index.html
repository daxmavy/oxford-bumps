Forecasting Oxford bumps racing: calibrated probabilities and their value in a prediction market

How accurately can bumps racing be forecast from the public record, and what are such forecasts worth? We forecast every crew's daily outcome over ten seasons of Summer Eights, test the forecasts in the Fantasy Bumps market, and find a ceiling set by the over-bumps that no pre-race information predicts.

This manuscript and the underlying analysis were produced by an automated system under human direction, and were revised through several rounds of independent automated peer review. It is shared as a reproducible research exercise rather than a peer-reviewed publication; all figures regenerate from the primary data and have been checked against the sources.

In brief. Bumps racing is decided by a chain of crews catching one another up a narrow river. We built a statistical forecast of every crew's daily result and entered it in the betting game run on the races. The forecasts are honest (when they say 30%, it happens about 30% of the time) and they beat the typical player every season. They almost never beat the very best player, though, because the game's biggest prizes ride on over-bumps: jumps of two or more places that hinge on how a separate contest up the river unfolds. Even a model that simulates the race itself cannot see them coming. A calibrated model extracts what the public record allows; the last few per cent belong to the river.

Abstract

In bumps racing, college crews start in a line and chase one another up a river; catching the crew ahead—a bump—exchanges the two crews' positions for the next day. We ask how accurately these outcomes can be forecast from the public record, and what such forecasts are worth in Fantasy Bumps, the prediction market run over the same races. Introduction

Bumps racing is the principal intercollegiate rowing format at Oxford and Cambridge. Because the river is too narrow for side-by-side racing, crews instead set off in a line at fixed spacing and pursue one another. A crew that catches the crew ahead scores a bump, and the two exchange starting positions for the following dayA crew that bumps may not continue, so by its own efforts a crew rises at most one place per day. Larger single-day gains, the over-bumps of two or more places, occur when a crew further ahead is removed from the order, leaving the chasing crew to pass the vacated positions. Summer Eights runs over four days; the related Torpids regatta uses different conventions and enters here only as a predictor.. A four-day regatta thus transforms an initial order into a final one through a sequence of coupled permutation updates, in which one crew's gain is by construction another's loss.

Our objective is twofold: to forecast each crew-day's outcome as accurately and as well-calibrated as possible (so that, when the model says 30%, the event happens about 30% of the time), and to perform well in Fantasy Bumps, the public prediction market run over the same races, in which several hundred entrants trade crews daily. The public record offers a great deal to work with—a century of results, each crew's seeding and roster continuity, the Torpids regatta raced a term earlier—but the format concentrates its uncertainty in awkward places. The first day pits each crew against its closest available match, and the largest single-day gains, the over-bumps, depend on a third crew leaving the order at the right moment.

Because the day's outcomes are mechanically coupled, the modelling choice is also not obvious: an independent per-crew classifier is flexible, but its stacked marginals need not form a valid joint over the day's permutation; a generative simulation of the race is coherent by construction, but rigid. Rather than presuppose either, we (i) grade both model classes on identical out-of-sample crew-days with strictly proper scoring rules and reliability analysis, with confidence intervals from a block bootstrap that respects the within-contest coupling; (ii) quantify the discrimination and calibration of the rare over-bump class, and test whether post-hoc recalibration changes accuracy or market value; (iii) characterise the market as the tournament it is, reporting the probability of beating the field's best human across selection strategies rather than a single expected-value operating point; and (iv) use the model-class comparison to test whether the market ceiling is methodological or informational.

The ceiling is not the model class

One objection remains: perhaps the ceiling reflects the choice of an independent per-crew classifier rather than the information available, since stacked marginals are not constrained to conserve places within a contest. The generative simulator (Methods) removes that objection by construction, because its draws are exact permutations of the field. In practice the incoherence turns out not to be empirically material. Discussion

The objective was to forecast bumps racing as accurately and as well-calibrated as the public record allows, and to turn those forecasts into performance in the market run over the same races. The first half succeeds. Out of sample the forecasts are calibrated across outcome classes, the skill traces to the relational structure of the format, and the one systematic defect we found, the under-sized over-bump tail, proved fixable by recalibration. The second half meets a hard ceiling: top decile of a field of several hundred in every season, never its best entrant.

What sets that ceiling? The separation between a strong forecaster and a perfect one is concentrated entirely in over-bumps, and our results locate where the difficulty lies. The relational model already knows which crews are over-bump candidates (it ranks them with AUC well above chance); what it cannot do is assign them enough probability, and that is a calibration defect we fixed. Yet fixing it barely moves the market outcome, because an over-bump requires a third crew to be removed at the right moment. The crew two places ahead might, for instance, catch its own target and pull over early, opening a gap the chasing crew can reach. Such a contingency turns on a different race unfolding a particular way, minutes up the river, which no pre-race information we have access to determines. After recalibration the strategy does occasionally back these crews, lifting its probability of beating the champion, but only into the low single figures. No selection strategy we tried—expected-value, variance-seeking, or concentrated—reliably wins, the champion's score is not an artefact of being the maximum of a large field, and the ceiling survives a change of model class: a coherent simulation of the race forecasts no better than the simple classifier and is equally blind to the over-bumps. We therefore read the ceiling as informational rather than methodological. The model extracts almost all of the publicly available signal; the residual is the unpredictable timing of over-bumps, supplemented by whatever private knowledge of current crew speed the strongest competitors hold.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The market evaluation rests on five seasons of one game at one venue, so its headline figures carry wide season-to-season ranges. We report those ranges rather than average them away. The over-bump analysis rests on — events, so its calibration estimates are correspondingly uncertain (we attach intervals throughout). The generative model is one specific mechanistic form; a different simulator might generate over-bumps more readily, though it would have to do so without harming the pairwise calibration that matches the common case. Membership of an Oxford openweight crew is also recorded only for the first boat. The honest summary is that, for this system, a flexible marginal model with a recalibrated tail is the better forecaster, and modelling the coupling explicitly does not help. The market's last few per cent are bought with information the river only reveals on the day.

Methods

Data

The unit is a crew-day: one college crew on one of the four racing days, with outcome the signed places it moved. Results come from two independently compiled records of every Oxford bumps race, which agree on 99.99% of the 20,617 races they jointly coverThe historical archive of A. Dudhia and the live results service bumps.live; records were joined across sources by season, gender, college and boat number. A small number of 2022 crews that could not be matched cause that season's perfect-foresight bound to be a slight underestimate.. The modern era (1996 onward, Summer Eights) is used for the marginal model; the generative model's roster graph and the Hilary-term Torpids predictor begin in 2007. Pre-1900 results, composite crews, and records with implausibly large moves are excluded. The market evaluation uses the live Fantasy Bumps order books, daily results and leaderboards for 2022–2026.

Two model classes

The marginal model is a histogram-based gradient-boosting classifier predicting, for each crew-day independently, a probability over six ordered classes (over-bumped, bumped, rowed over, bump, over-bump, multi-place over-bump). It uses each crew's own history and seeding plus relational features describing the crews immediately ahead and behind, recomputed from the current order each day; hyperparameters were fixed in advance. (Additional inputs—Torpids form, individual-rower ratings, qualification times—were tested in earlier work and found non-additive, and are excluded.) The coherent joint model assigns each crew a latent skill in boat-gaps of pace (a Bayesian estimate from seeding, Torpids, last year and Blue-Boat membership, updated within the week by the observed earlier days) and simulates the coupled catch process. Each crew rows at its skill plus daily noise, chases the nearest crew ahead, and both stop on a catch, so over-bumps arise endogenously when a crew further ahead is removed. Monte-Carlo over the skill posterior and the day noise yields a coherent six-class marginal per crew-day. The day-noise scale is calibrated so the simulated pairwise bump rate matches the data.

Leak-free evaluation

Both models are graded under an identical walk-forward: to forecast year Y each is fitted only on data strictly before Y, and within a race week day d conditions on the realised results of days < d (never the current day). For the marginal model, hyperparameters were fixed a priori; for the joint model, the skill hyperparameters are refit each year on earlier seasons. Proper scores are the multiclass Brier score and the ranked probability score; skill scores are relative to a climatology refit each step (day-only, and a stronger variant additionally conditioned on river-position quintile). Calibration is summarised by reliability curves, the expected calibration error, and the Cox calibration slope (the coefficient of a logistic regression of outcome on forecast logit; unity is ideal). Discrimination is the one-versus-rest ROC AUC. Confidence intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a block bootstrap of 1,000 replicates that resamples whole contests (year × gender × day × division), the independent unit given the within-day coupling; paired model comparisons resample the same clusters.

Recalibration and the coherence diagnostic

Because the walk-forward predictions are already out of sample, the marginal model's probabilities are recalibrated leak-free without retraining: to recalibrate year Y, a per-class logistic (Platt) map is fit on the out-of-sample predictions of the test years strictly before Y and applied to year Y, then the six classes are renormalised; the earliest year keeps its raw probabilities. The coherence diagnostic is the per-contest sum of expected signed places moved. Because class probabilities are mapped to places through class-mean magnitudes, even a perfect forecast leaves a nonzero binning residual, so we report the marginal model's residual against that perfect-foresight floor rather than against zero.

Market reconstruction and decision frontier

Fantasy Bumps prices each crew by its river position and pays a holding when its crew rows over or advancesA row-over returns 7% of a crew's value and each place gained a further 14% (so a single bump returns 21% and a two-place over-bump 35%); the head crew's row-over returns 17.5%. The reconstructed engine reproduces the platform's published payouts exactly. Participants manage a men's and a women's boat, each endowed with 1,000 units holding nine shares, rebuilt daily.. Each day a strategy chooses the nine shares per boat that maximise its objective subject to the budget, solved as an integer program; the expected return uses within-class conditional mean places, so a multi-place over-bump is valued at its empirical +3.6 rather than the class edge (these two open-class magnitudes are estimated over the full sample; with only 62 multi-place over-bumps the effect on the headline figures is negligible). We report a grid of strategies—expected value, three degrees of variance-seeking, and concentration—for each model. The expected-value rule maximises expected, not realised, wealth, and because the budget binds it is not the optimal policy for a contest decided by an extreme order statistic. To probe that gap we report a within-model diagnostic: holding each strategy's realised-path picks fixed, we redraw their returns from the model's own class probabilities and estimate the chance the resulting final wealth exceeds the (fixed) champion score. This is a variance diagnostic, not a true tournament-win probability—it neither lets the picks adapt nor models the opposing field—so we read it alongside, not above, the realised season ranks.
